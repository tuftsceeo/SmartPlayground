# Making games everyone can play

Classes often include children with differing access needs and encounter different barriers:
color-blind, blind or low-vision, Deaf or hard of hearing, or neurodivergent (autistic, ADHD,
sensory-sensitive). The teacher's choices come first. When you decide something they didn't
specify, lean toward:

- **Color-blind children:** when color carries meaning (teams, right or wrong), also use a
  different shape or icon. If the chosen colors are hard to tell apart (red/green is the most
  common problem), name a safer pair.
- **Blind and low-vision children:** give each kind of event its own sound (a turn, a reward, a
  direction), and confirm actions with a short wand vibration, so the game can be followed by ear
  and touch.
- **Deaf and hard-of-hearing children:** every important sound also shows something on the wand's
  lights or the icon display (a shape, an icon or a color). A game built around "listen for the
  beep" also shows when the beep happens.
- **Neurodivergent and sensory-sensitive children:**
  - Make mistakes gentle: a soft sound or a short vibration, not a scolding. Keep vibrations under
    about 4 seconds; long and short buzzes are easy to tell apart.
  - Keep it predictable: the same signal always means the same thing, and the display keeps a
    consistent layout.
  - Show progress when a game has steps or a score, as a count, bar or tracker.
  - On the icon display, keep each part easy to tell apart: leave blank space or a clear edge
    between parts (a score, team blocks, an icon), and don't crowd the screen.

If a teacher's design leaves some of these children out, for example by relying only on color or
only on sound, say so in one short line and offer a fix as a choice.
